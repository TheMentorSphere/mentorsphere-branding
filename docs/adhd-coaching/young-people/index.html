Could coaching help?

You might recognise some of this

Every young person is different, and coaching starts from what is actually happening for yours. Parents and carers may get in touch when a capable young person finds that school, home routines or everyday expectations do not seem to fit the way they work.

A formal ADHD diagnosis is not required to enquire. If your young person is diagnosed, waiting for an assessment, or you are exploring whether ADHD may be relevant, suitability can be discussed during the free introduction.

Situations coaching can explore

  • Starting homework, revision or everyday tasks feels much harder than it looks from the outside
  • Mornings, transitions or keeping track of belongings regularly become pressure points
  • Frustration, setbacks or feeling different are starting to affect confidence
  • Conversations about routines keep ending in conflict or overwhelm at home
  • Your young person wants more independence and would benefit from strategies of their own
  • You would like to understand ADHD better and feel more confident supporting your young person

What coaching can support

Practical support, shaped around your young person

There is no fixed programme. Priorities are agreed with your young person and reviewed as coaching goes on, so the focus stays useful to them.

Getting started with less pressure

Explore what makes starting difficult, then try manageable first steps, prompts and routines together.

Routines that fit real life

Work on planning, transitions, organising belongings or study routines in a way that respects energy and current capacity.

Emotions, confidence and self-understanding

Make space to understand ADHD, recognise strengths and talk about frustration, setbacks or difficult moments without shame.

Growing independence at a realistic pace

Develop practical ways to ask for help, express needs and take more ownership, with the right support around them.

Confidentiality

Your young person's voice stays central

Coaching should feel like a space that genuinely belongs to your young person. How privacy works is explained clearly before coaching begins.

A private space

Young people are given age-appropriate privacy. Session content is not automatically passed on, and I do not routinely report back everything that is discussed.

Sharing what helps

Where sharing a strategy or concern would help, we talk about what could be shared and agree an appropriate approach with your young person.

Clear limits

Confidentiality is not absolute. Information may need to be shared where there is a safeguarding concern, a serious risk of harm or a legal requirement. These limits are explained at the start.

Parents and carers

Two ways parents and carers can be involved

Coaching for you as a parent or carer

You can have ADHD coaching yourself as a parent or carer supporting a young person with ADHD. Sessions focus on your goals, such as understanding ADHD and executive function, communication, routines and supportive strategies at home. This can stand on its own; your young person does not need to be receiving coaching too.

Your coaching is kept separate from your young person's. If they also have sessions, what they share there is not automatically passed on to you and stays within the confidentiality arrangements agreed with them.

Involvement when your young person is the coaching client

When your young person is being coached, I generally recommend that around one in every four sessions involves a parent or guardian. This is a general recommendation, not a requirement, and coaching does not depend on a parent attending a fixed proportion of sessions.

The balance is agreed according to your young person's age, needs, preferences and circumstances. Involvement can take place separately or as part of a joint session, and how it fits alongside your young person's sessions is agreed in advance.

A parent session is not a report card. It is space to understand how to support your young person, adapt the environment and find realistic compromises, so that progress does not rest on them making every change alone.

Parent sessions can explore

  • Understanding how ADHD shows up for your young person
  • Supportive routines and co-regulation
  • Reducing conflict or overwhelm at home
  • Compassionate boundaries
  • Supporting growing independence

Working alongside school

School involvement, where it helps

With appropriate consent, coaching can include proportionate communication with your young person's school, such as speaking with relevant staff, sharing agreed strategies or contributing to a joined-up approach.

School involvement is optional. What is shared is agreed with you and your young person in advance, and private session content is not automatically passed to school staff.

How coaching works

Small, realistic steps, reviewed together

1

Start with the person

We talk about what matters to your young person, what is already working and where support might help.

2

Choose a manageable focus

Sessions use conversation and practical strategies rather than a one-size-fits-all programme.

3

Try, reflect and adjust

We agree a realistic next step and revisit it, without treating a difficult week as failure.

Practical details

  • Sessions are one-to-one and online, using an approved platform such as Lessonspace or Google Meet.
  • Coaching sessions normally last 60 minutes.
  • A verbal-first approach keeps written admin and overwhelm low.
  • Breaks, camera-off arrangements or shorter sessions can be discussed where appropriate.
  • A parent or guardian normally completes or approves the initial service agreement.

Common questions

Questions you may have

Does my young person need an ADHD diagnosis?

No. You can get in touch while exploring ADHD or waiting for an assessment. The introduction focuses on needs, suitability and the support you are looking for. Coaching cannot diagnose ADHD.

What if my young person is unsure about coaching?

That is understandable. The introduction is a chance to find out what coaching involves. Your young person's voice and willingness matter, and coaching is not presented as something being done to them. You can also have coaching for yourself as a parent or carer, whether or not your young person decides to take part.

What ages is this coaching for?

Direct coaching for young people is for ages 10 to 17. Parents and guardians supporting a young person with ADHD can also have coaching for themselves, as described above. Coaching for adults aged 18 and over is explained in the ADHD coaching overview.

Can we start without a package?

Yes. A single self-funded session is £70 for 60 minutes. Packages are optional.

Is coaching the same as therapy or an ADHD assessment?

No. Coaching is practical and strategy-based. It does not replace diagnosis, medical treatment, therapy or crisis support, although it can sit alongside them.

What coaching is and is not

ADHD coaching is practical, reflective support. It is not therapy, clinical diagnosis, medical treatment or crisis support, and it does not replace healthcare. The MentorSphere cannot diagnose ADHD. Where a need falls outside coaching, this will be discussed honestly and appropriate signposting may be suggested.

Immediate danger: call 999. The MentorSphere is not an emergency or crisis service.
